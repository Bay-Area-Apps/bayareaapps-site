PROJECT PLANNING

A clear scope makes a better project

Agree on what gets built, how you review it, and what you receive at handoff.

By Bay Area Apps · Updated October 28, 2026 · Project planning guide

Turn features into outcomes

A feature name can hide several different expectations. “Booking” might mean a simple request form, live calendar availability, payment collection, or staff scheduling. Specify which behavior is included. A statement such as “customers request a date, and the business confirms it later” is much clearer than a single feature label.

Define what finished means

For each important journey, describe the expected result. A valid inquiry should be saved, the customer should receive a reference and access route, and the business should be able to find it. A failed submission should show a useful message rather than pretending the request succeeded. These acceptance points give both sides something concrete to review.

Use review milestones

Choose moments when feedback will be most useful: an early screen review, a working version of the main journey, and a final check against the proposal. Decide who provides feedback and consolidate it where possible. A list of specific changes is easier to act on than several conflicting messages about the same screen.

Separate included work from changes

A request that expands the original outcome can change the price and timeline. That is easier to discuss when the initial scope is written down. Keep a short record of approved changes and their effect. The goal is to let the project evolve deliberately while keeping both sides aware of the commitment.

Know what you will receive

Discuss source code, design files, content, setup instructions, and service accounts before work begins. Clarify who owns the domain and which third-party services are required. Any license restrictions or conditions on transferring deliverables belong in the agreement. Make sure someone other than the original developer can understand the basic setup.

Agree on the period after delivery

A handoff should explain how to report a defect, what support is included, and how new features are handled. Continuing maintenance is a separate decision unless the proposal includes it. Keep account recovery and access ownership with the appropriate business, and never exchange passwords in a public project thread.

A reviewable scope: replace “booking works” with observable checks

For an illustrative request-based service, the customer chooses a preferred day and the owner decides whether to accept. A submitted request is not a reservation. The following checks describe that distinction in a way both parties can test.

Ask for a handoff you can use

  • Ownership: domain registrar, repository, hosting, and service accounts, with the business as the appropriate owner.
  • Operation: how to publish a content change, find a request, recover access, and report an issue.
  • Continuity: a documented backup and restore process for stored business records, plus a way to export them where supported.
  • Costs: a list of recurring services, who pays, and which usage can increase charges. Do not treat a development quote as the total operating cost.
  • Limitations: supported devices, known issues, and which notifications, integrations, and offline behaviors were actually verified.

Download the planning and acceptance worksheet. Keep it with the proposal and complete each check against a working build. Screenshots are useful for appearance; a successful end-to-end journey is needed to verify behavior.
